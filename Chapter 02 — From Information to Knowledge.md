# Chapter 2 — From Information to Knowledge

The internet contains enormous amounts of information. But information alone is not knowledge.

A document may say:

> “Company A acquired Company B in 2025.”

For a computer, this is mostly text. For a knowledge system, it can be represented as connected concepts:

**Company A → acquired → Company B**

But even this relationship is incomplete. We also need to know where it came from, when it was published, and what evidence supports it.

## Knowledge as Claims

Open Knowledge Protocol treats a **claim** as a basic unit of knowledge.

A claim connects entities through a relationship:

**Subject → Relationship → Object**

For example:

**Earth → orbits → Sun**

Each claim can carry additional information such as:

- Source
- Evidence
- Publisher
- Timestamp
- Confidence
- Verification status

This allows a claim to be understood and evaluated rather than simply stored.

## Knowledge Is Connected

Knowledge rarely exists in isolation.

A company has founders. A person works for an organization. A research paper supports a claim. Another paper may challenge it.

Together, these connections form a **knowledge graph**.

As more knowledge is added, existing information becomes more useful because new relationships can be discovered across it.

## Knowledge Can Change

What we know today may change tomorrow.

New evidence may support a claim, challenge it, or replace it with a better explanation. Open Knowledge Protocol therefore does not treat knowledge as a collection of permanent facts.

Instead, it preserves claims, their evidence, their relationships, and how they evolve over time.

The goal is not simply to store information.

**The goal is to build a connected and traceable representation of what we know, how we know it, and how that knowledge changes.**
