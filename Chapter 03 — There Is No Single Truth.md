# Chapter 3 — There Is No Single Truth

Knowledge is not always a collection of simple facts.

For many questions, different sources reach different conclusions. Research can conflict. Historical accounts can disagree. Economic and political claims can depend on interpretation, assumptions, or new evidence.

A knowledge system should be able to represent this disagreement.

## Store Claims, Not Absolute Truth

Open Knowledge Protocol does not decide whether a claim is universally true.

Instead, it records the claim together with the information needed to evaluate it:

- Who made the claim?
- What evidence supports it?
- Which sources agree with it?
- Which sources challenge it?
- Has newer evidence changed our understanding?

Two conflicting claims can therefore exist in the graph at the same time.

## Evidence Builds Trust

Consider two claims:

**Claim A:** Policy X increased employment.

**Claim B:** Policy X had no significant effect on employment.

Instead of choosing one and deleting the other, the graph can connect each claim to its studies, data, authors, supporting arguments, and counter-evidence.

Applications using the graph can then decide how to evaluate those claims based on their own trust rules.

## A Real-World Example

Consider the question:

**Does drinking coffee reduce the risk of heart disease?**

One research study may find that moderate coffee consumption is associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease. Another may find little benefit, while another may identify increased risk for certain groups.

A traditional database may try to store a single answer.

Open Knowledge Protocol can store all of these findings separately and connect each one to its research paper, authors, dataset, publication date, and supporting evidence.

As new studies are published, they can be added without removing the earlier findings.

The graph therefore does not need to declare:

**“Coffee is good for your heart.”**

Instead, it can represent **what different sources claim and the evidence behind those claims.**

## The Protocol Does Not Define Truth

This distinction is fundamental to Open Knowledge Protocol.

The network can confirm that a claim exists and that its history has not been altered. It does not make the claim true.

Truth should emerge from evidence, verification, challenge, and continued discovery—not from a database, blockchain, organization, or vote.

**Open Knowledge Protocol does not create a single version of truth. It creates an open system for understanding why something should—or should not—be trusted.**
